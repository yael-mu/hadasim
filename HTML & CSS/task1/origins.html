How It Started

The story of the Titanic began in the early twentieth century, when ocean travel was becoming faster, more popular, and more competitive. Large ships carried passengers, immigrants, mail, and goods between Europe and North America. Two major British shipping companies, White Star Line and Cunard Line, competed to build impressive ocean liners.

White Star Line decided to create a new class of enormous ships. The goal was not simply to make the ships fast. The company wanted them to be comfortable, luxurious, and impressive. Three sister ships were planned: Olympic, Titanic, and Britannic. The Titanic was designed to be one of the largest passenger ships ever built at the time.

Construction began in 1909 at the Harland and Wolff shipyard in Belfast, Ireland. Thousands of workers helped build the ship. The work involved enormous quantities of steel, machinery, wood, glass, and other materials. The ship was more than 260 meters long and contained restaurants, lounges, a gymnasium, libraries, cabins, and other facilities.

The Titanic was divided into three main passenger classes. First-class passengers had luxurious rooms and elegant dining areas. Second-class passengers had comfortable accommodation, while third-class passengers had simpler rooms. The ship therefore reflected many of the social differences of its time.

Building a Floating City

On May 31, 1911, the Titanic was launched into the water for the first time. Launching was only one stage of the project. Engineers and workers still had to install engines, boilers, electrical systems, furniture, decorations, and safety equipment. The final preparations took months.

When completed, the Titanic was a remarkable example of early twentieth-century engineering. Its size and luxurious interiors attracted enormous public attention. Newspapers described the ship with excitement, and many people were eager to experience the maiden voyage.

The ship left Southampton, England, on April 10, 1912. It stopped at Cherbourg in France and Queenstown in Ireland before beginning its Atlantic crossing toward New York City. Hundreds of passengers were excited to begin their journey. Some were wealthy travelers going on holiday, while others were immigrants hoping to start a new life in America.

The First Voyage

The maiden voyage was intended to demonstrate the ship's comfort and reliability. Passengers ate in dining rooms, walked on deck, talked with other travelers, and settled into their cabins. Everything appeared normal as the Titanic crossed the Atlantic.

Yet the voyage would soon become one of the most famous disasters in history. On the night of April 14, the ship struck an iceberg. The collision damaged the hull, and water began entering several compartments. During the early hours of April 15, the Titanic sank beneath the North Atlantic.

Explore the Route

The Titanic's voyage began in Southampton and continued through stops in France and Ireland before heading west toward New York.
