People Behind the Story

The Titanic was not only a ship. It was a temporary community containing more than two thousand people. Passengers and crew came from different countries, professions, and social backgrounds. Their individual experiences make the story much more than a story about a shipwreck.

Some people were responsible for operating the ship, while others were traveling for business, immigration, holidays, or family reasons. The disaster affected everyone differently, and the surviving accounts became an important part of the historical record.

Captain Edward Smith

Captain Edward Smith was an experienced British sailor and a senior captain of the White Star Line. He had commanded many ships before the Titanic. He was known as an experienced and respected captain and was scheduled to retire after the Titanic's maiden voyage.

During the emergency, Smith was responsible for the overall response. The collision with the iceberg created a rapidly developing crisis. Officers and crew members worked to launch lifeboats and organize passengers, but the ship could not remain afloat.

Thomas Andrews

Thomas Andrews was an Irish shipbuilder and managing director of Harland and Wolff's design department. Because he was closely involved with the Titanic's construction, he traveled on the maiden voyage to observe the ship and identify possible improvements.

After the collision, Andrews examined the damage and understood that the ship was in serious danger. His role during the emergency has become one of the best-known stories associated with the disaster.

J. Bruce Ismay

J. Bruce Ismay was the chairman and managing director of the White Star Line. He traveled on the Titanic during its maiden voyage. His actions after the disaster became controversial, and newspapers closely followed his story. His experience demonstrates how the Titanic disaster continued to be debated long after the ship sank.

Passengers and Crew

The people aboard the Titanic included wealthy first-class travelers, second-class passengers, third-class passengers, families, children, workers, and hundreds of crew members. Third-class passengers included immigrants traveling toward a new life in North America. Crew members included engineers, cooks, stewards, officers, sailors, and many others.

The different groups did not experience the voyage in the same way. Their cabins, dining areas, and daily routines differed according to class and occupation. During the evacuation, however, the disaster created an emergency that affected the entire ship.
